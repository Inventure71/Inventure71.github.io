Dream2Detect

Can a model trained entirely on AI-generated package photos learn to judge damage in real ones? I built Dream2Detect to find out — and to find out honestly.

Damage scale

Ten bands, from intact to destroyed

I didn't just prompt for damaged boxes

Every image was generated from thirteen structured fields — damage type, box shape, tape, background, camera angle, lighting — deliberately varied so the model couldn't cheat by learning the scenery instead of the damage. Then every single candidate went through human review before it earned a spot in training.

  • In one 800-image review pass, 623 were kept as generated and 177 relabeled by hand — the label is what I saw, not what I asked for.
  • A second generation round deliberately targeted the real-world cases earlier models kept getting wrong.

The modeling story

Two ways I tried to crack it

From scratch, eight times over

I started with custom CNNs and no pretrained weights, then rebuilt them across eight versions. Each one answered a single question — cleaner labels, higher resolution, stricter splits, ordinal losses — and on identical data the real-photo error fell from 2.66 down to 2.11 bands, landing within one band of correct about 39% of the time.

  • V1 existed just to prove the synthetic labels were learnable at all.
  • I kept the dead-ends, too — longer training and a GroupNorm swap both made things worse.

Then pretrained backbones won

The strongest models paired a ConvNeXt backbone with an Earth Mover's Distance loss — one that punishes a guess three bands off far more than a near miss — blended 60/40 with a SAM-cropped variant. On the same synthetic data, they jumped from 39% to 64% within one band on real photos — a 25-point gain from better visual representations alone.

  • 21% were dead-on out of ten bands — about double random chance — but "within one" is what a real severity call actually needs.
  • Averaging the two views beat either model on its own.

Kept honest on purpose

It's easy to fool yourself here, so the splits were built to stop prompt-family leakage, and I never let a flattering synthetic number stand in for real-photo transfer. The from-scratch and pretrained tracks were reported as two separate answers, not one tidy line.

  • On synthetic holdout the from-scratch models cleared roughly twice random chance.
  • Real transfer stayed the honest bottleneck — and that's the point.

A floor, not a ceiling

Here's the part I actually care about: every time I tightened the loop — cleaner labels, stricter splits, better losses, pretrained representations — the real-world number climbed, from a from-scratch model barely clearing random to 64% within one band. Synthetic-only transfer was still the honest bottleneck, and I won't pretend otherwise. But nothing here reads like a dead end; it reads like the opposite. With more data and tighter management, this loop keeps closing the gap — 64% is where I stopped, not where it tops out.
